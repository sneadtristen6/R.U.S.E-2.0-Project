## 6. RUSE Guard: our anti-cheat (required)

Why: to stop cheating in R.U.S.E. multiplayer and co-op matches played with copies our apps build. Mods can change how the game plays. So each copy whose mods change play gets a join code that you compare with the people you play with. A copy built with a cheat or a test tool checks that every player in the match has the same build, and gives the match up if not. In multiplayer and co-op matches, RUSE Guard also looks for known cheat tools running on your PC. And every copy's watcher shows on screen when a match sees a spawn order or a sudden jump in a side's money.

Why it's required: RUSE Guard is required to use our apps. R.U.S.E. doesn't have an effective anti-cheat, which is why RUSE Guard is needed: it helps keep cheats out of multiplayer, and fair matches matter to us and to every player you meet. We rely on what data-protection law calls a "legitimate interest": fair matches for every player, which needs no permission from you as long as your privacy doesn't outweigh it (section 11). That's why it isn't a tick box like Bug Watch: the Studio and the Launcher as we publish them always include it. You can still object at any time (section 11).

- Where it acts: RUSE Guard is in every modded copy the apps build, and the Launcher's Vanilla builds such a copy too, with no mods (section 10). Its lock and its program check act only in multiplayer and co-op matches, and so would its match records, once they start (below). Its watcher, also in every modded copy, watches multiplayer, co-op and skirmish matches against the computer: it can show a notice on your own screen when a side's money jumps by 400 or more at once, or when a unit is made by a spawn order. In a multiplayer match, every player's screen can show it. The watcher changes nothing in the match and sends nothing. Only the Launcher sends anything for RUSE Guard; the Studio never does, and its Test in game runs no program check.
- The list: RUSE Guard's list of known cheat tools comes inside the app, as a plain file you can read, and changes only with an app update. Nothing is downloaded for it.
- The program check: in a multiplayer or co-op match started from the Launcher, RUSE Guard looks every 30 seconds at the names of the programs running on your PC, and keeps only names that are on its list. To know when such a match is on, the Launcher reads the game's own error output while the game runs: it keeps only RUSE Guard's short lines that say a match is on or has ended, and drops every other line as soon as it reads it, never keeping or sending it (that output can also hold Steam's own lines, one of them with your Steam account number). If the game doesn't say that a match has ended, the check stops two minutes after the game last said it was on. If a listed program was running during a match, the Launcher sends our server a short record after the game: your username and RUSE Guard key, when the match started and ended, whether it was multiplayer or co-op, and the names of the listed programs found. Nothing else goes in: no replay, no files and nothing more about the match. If no listed program was running, nothing is sent.
- The join code: for a modded copy whose mods change how the game plays, the Launcher shows a join code. It stands for the copy's game version, its mods with their versions, and what they change in how the game plays. You compare join codes with the people you play with. We don't receive them today; a match record (below) would carry them.
- Copies built with a cheat or test tool: such a copy plays multiplayer and co-op only with players whose build is the same (the same game version and the same mods, in the same versions). About a second into each such match, it tells every player's game a short code for its build (its build code), made from the game's version, the mods with their versions in their order, and what they change in how the game plays. It holds no name or account. If every player has the same build, the match is built to go on; nobody has played such a match with two people yet. If not, it gives up the match a few seconds in.

  In a multiplayer match that isn't ranked, it then stops the game on purpose at that match's end-of-match screen: the game crashes to the desktop. The game's own crash reporter can then send its report to the game's maker, and that report carries RUSE Guard's line saying a cheat copy was stopped. In co-op and ranked matches it only gives up the match. When the Launcher started the game and is still open when the game stops, it takes only RUSE Guard's line from the game's crash report on your PC and sends our server a short note that a cheat copy was stopped, under your username and RUSE Guard key, with when the crash report was written and when the note was made. The line says only the kind of match and your side's number in it; any Steam ID, long number or path is taken out. Our apps never send the crash report itself. The note opens no review and decides nothing; it's listed for sneadtristen6 to see. No note is sent if the Launcher was closed before the game stopped, or if the game was started from the Studio or by hand. On Linux and the Steam Deck, whether the note is sent isn't known yet.

  Honest copies are never stopped.

- Your RUSE Guard key: the first time the Launcher makes a RUSE Guard record on your PC, it makes a random key there. A code made from this key (its public half) goes with each record, so we can tell your records apart from those of someone with the same username, and check they weren't changed. It says nothing else about you. Settings shows a short code made from it, "Your RUSE Guard code": send it with a privacy request, and we can find your own RUSE Guard records. Nothing more is sent for it.
- Not active yet: match records. They need a record of the match that the game can't write today. Before they start, we'll update this text, and the apps will show it to you again. When they start, a match record will carry: your username and RUSE Guard key; your side; your copy's codes; checks of the game files your copy changed, and regular checks that each player's game stays the same; your mods with their checks; which script commands your mods tie to keys (never what you press); for a developer's test copy, the developer's key; the names of any listed cheat tools found running; when the match started and ended; and the newest replay saved on your PC during or just after the match, with its file name. A replay holds every order each player gave in the match, and its file name and contents can include your and other players' in-game names.
- What it never does: it never collects your screen or your keystrokes, never looks inside other programs while they run, and never sends us your files, apart from the replay a match record would carry. Of the game's crash reports, it reads only its own line about a cheat copy it stopped (above), on your PC.
- Developers' test copies: the apps are built to let a developer's test copy into a match with a special key from us, the one way past the cheat-copy rule. None works yet: the apps can't check such a key today.
- Decisions about you: no automatic bans. No decision about cheating is made by a machine alone: sneadtristen6 looks at the evidence, and at the replay when there is one, and decides. Anthropic's Claude, an AI assistant, helps him read RUSE Guard's records, under Anthropic's own terms; the decisions are his. A record that names a listed cheat tool opens a review for him, which decides nothing until he does. You can appeal within 30 days of a decision (section 17); sneadtristen6 is the only person who reviews cases, so he also decides appeals himself. The one automatic rule is the cheat-copy rule above. It follows from the copy you chose to build, and it never touches your Steam account or your installed game.
- What a decision means today: if cheating is confirmed, we keep that decision under your username on our list of flagged players, and use it when we look at later matches. Because two players can share a username, we also look at which RUSE Guard key the records came with. Our RUSE Guard server keeps that list, each flagged username with the RUSE Guard key its records first came with, and tells only that player's own Launcher that it's flagged. The apps don't act on a flag yet. If a later version does, for example to keep a flagged player's modded copies out of multiplayer through R.U.S.E. 2.0, this text will say so first. If an appeal is upheld, the decision is marked overturned, and the username comes off the list.
- How long records are kept, and your rights: section 11.

## 7. Bug Watch: help us find and fix bugs (optional, recommended)

Why: to make the Studio and the Launcher work better, and to fix crashes sooner: instead of waiting for someone to report a crash, we see it and what led to it.

On what basis: only with your OK (your consent, section 11). It's off unless you tick it. Saving or changing your username never turns it on or off. Ticking it changes nothing else in the apps, except what the Launcher tells you after a game crash (below). When the apps show these terms again after a change: if Bug Watch is on, its box asks "Keep Bug Watch on?", ticked; leave the tick to keep it on, or untick it to turn it off. If it's off, the Bug Watch box comes again, unticked.

While it's on, the Studio and the Launcher send our server:

- once a day, the counts for each day that has ended:
  - the app and its version; the day; whether you use Windows 10 or 11; the app's language; the game's version; whether RUSE Guard is built in; which version of these terms you accepted;
  - how often each part of the app was used and how long things took; what went wrong and where (in our own code only; when a Studio screen's own script fails, the screen's name and the kind of error, never its words, file or line); for each long task, such as a mod build, how many times it finished or failed, and how many builds found mistakes in the mods;
  - a list of that day's failures, at most 20, each with its code (which place in our code), how far a Play or a Test in game got, the shape of the mod set (how many mods from our list, mod folders and mod files of your own, as counts), and beside it only numbers that name no one: the Windows error number (such as disk full), the web server's answer (such as 404), the game's exit number, a count and a second code;
  - how many updates were offered, installed or failed; how many times the app crashed; which troubleshooter checks found a problem;
  - which mods from our list you had on; how many minutes you played in all, and how many with each of those mods;
  - how many mods of your own you had, and what kinds of things they hold, such as units, maps, missions or scripts (counted by kind, never named, and never their words, paths or files);
- right away, when an app crashes: the kind of error and where in our code;
- right away, after a game the app started has crashed, technical details of where and how it crashed:
  - the game's error code; the name of the file it stopped in (this can be part of Windows, a driver, or another program loaded into the game, such as an overlay), where in it the game stopped, and up to 16 places in the game that led there;
  - for a memory error, what the game was trying to do (read, write or run) and the place in memory;
  - for a script error, the game's own error text, with paths on a drive cut down to file names, your user folder's name hidden (the names of folders inside it can stay), and Steam account numbers and other long numbers taken out (the game's own script names stay); how many earlier errors the game had skipped; and the map (one of the game's own maps by its name, a mod's map only as "a mod's map");
  - the unit the game was making, when the apps can read it from the game's crash report (one of the game's own units by its number, a mod's unit only as "a mod's unit");
  - which mods were on, what kinds of things your own mods hold (counted by kind), and which version of these terms you accepted.

Bug Watch uses only the game's own crash reports. Of another program's report, it reads only the names of the files it lists, to tell that it isn't the game's; nothing of it is sent.

Each send carries your username and your install ID, a random code made on your PC when you first chose your username, whether or not you tick Bug Watch. Your report code, which Settings shows while Bug Watch is on, is the first 8 characters of your install ID.

Our apps never send your files or the game's crash report files themselves, and Bug Watch sends no Steam account number. (The game's own crash reporter can send its own report to the game's maker: section 3.) The game's error text can include names from the game or from a mod's scripts. Please don't use your real name as your username (section 9).

Crashes we know: with Bug Watch on, if the game crashes in one of the ways we know, the Launcher tells you which unit the game was making and why, in your language. That explanation is only shown to you, never sent; Bug Watch's report carries the unit only as listed above (a number, or "a mod's unit"). To know those crashes, the apps download our list of them from our Bug Watch server when a game starts, unless the list on your PC is less than a day old, and once more at a crash if there's no usable list. They download it only while Bug Watch is on, keep it on your PC, use it only if it carries our signature, and check your crash report against it on your PC. The download carries nothing about you. Without the list (for example the first time, or without the internet), the Launcher still tells you the game crashed, but not the unit or the reason. Unticking Bug Watch deletes the list from your PC.

With Bug Watch on or off: if a game the Launcher started crashes, the Launcher shows you a short crash code, made on your PC from where the game stopped. It isn't sent anywhere. A Copy code button copies it. If you want help, send the code and a screenshot of the message to XUL on Discord (XUL6x3) or on GitHub, and we look it up in our own list. The code says only where the game stopped.

Settings shows you today's counts word for word, as they would be sent, and the names of the reports waiting to go. Reports that can't be sent wait on your PC for up to 30 days, then are dropped. While a game the app started is running, Bug Watch also keeps a small note about it on your PC, so that at its next start the app can tell whether the game crashed: when it started and was last seen running, its mods (ours by name and version, others only as a count), and where the copy and the game are installed (a Windows folder can include your Windows user name). It's never sent, and it's deleted after 30 days, or at once when you untick Bug Watch.

Who sees it: our developers. It's stored for us by Cloudflare on our Bug Watch server. When it's switched on, our server also posts notes on the problems we track to a private GitHub repository, a space on GitHub that only our developers can open. The notes never hold a username or a report code. Our developers also use Anthropic's Claude, an AI assistant, to help read some Bug Watch crash reports, under Anthropic's own terms.

How long: each report is deleted from our server at the daily clean-up after it has been there 7 days, so 7 to 8 days after it arrives. Our developers can mark the report of a very severe crash to keep it while they fix that crash; a marked report stays until they take the mark off, the day that crash is fixed, and then goes at the next daily clean-up. What stays after that, with no username, install ID or report code in it: daily totals for each app version (like "12 mod builds failed on version 0.9.9 that day") and the day each version and each kind of error was first seen, with no time limit; and our server's notes on the problems it finds (which part of an app, which mod from our list, where a crash happened, and how often), until 3 days after we close that problem. For the first 7 days, on a day when only one person used a version, its totals are that one person's counts; after that, a version or a mod from our list that fewer than 3 installs used that day is kept only as "(other)". The notes on GitHub stay until we delete them. Cloudflare keeps a history of our databases for 7 days, so what we delete stays in that history for up to 7 more days. Copies on sneadtristen6's computer: section 11, "Where it's kept".

Untick it any time in Settings (or in the Bug Watch box when the apps show these terms). From then on nothing is counted or sent, and the apps delete Bug Watch's counts and the reports waiting to go. Your username and install ID stay on your PC, because RUSE Guard uses your username. Nothing made before you unticked it is sent later, even if you tick it again. Unticking tells our server nothing: what already reached it is deleted as above. To have it deleted sooner, email us your report code (Settings shows it only while Bug Watch is on, so note it before you untick), or your username if you don't have the code: we find your install's reports by it and delete them. The totals and notes above stay. Unticking doesn't undo what was sent before.

Where WebView2 is missing, the installers (a ticked box you can untick), the apps when they start ("Install it now?") and Settings ("Install WebView2") offer Microsoft's WebView2 installer, which comes with the apps; if you say yes, it downloads WebView2 from Microsoft and installs it, under Microsoft's terms. - Buttons that open your browser: our GitHub pages and Discord, a mod's homepage, Microsoft's WebView2 download, blender.org, and the credit pages of era units.
